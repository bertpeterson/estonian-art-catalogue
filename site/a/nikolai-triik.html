Nikolai Triik

1884–1940 · Estonian painter (1884–1940)
Allee galerii; E-Kunstisalong; Eesti Ajaloomuuseum; Eesti Kunstimuuseum; Haus Galerii; Hiiumaa Muuseumid

movement Expressionism · trained at Académie Colarossi · trained at Atelier School of Ants Laikmaa · trained at Saint Petersburg Stieglitz State Academy of Art and Design

Painter, printmaker and pedagogue. At the beginning of his creative path he drew pictures with political content, including caricatures (under the pseudonym Vaikne), for Estonian satirical publications. The figure "The Torch-Bearer" on the cover of the first "Noor-Eesti" album was the first to attract attention. He made illustrations and design based on Art Nouveau-stylised ornament and symbolic imagery for G. Suits's poetry collection "Tulemaa" (1913) and for the fifth "Noor-Eesti" album (1915). While staying in Norway he focused on landscape painting ("Decorative Norwegian Landscape", 1908). His early painterly work shows the influence of symbolism, especially in compositions with national-romantic legendary subjects and depicting heroic battle scenes. Triik was one of the most esteemed portraitists, emphasising spirituality, inner dignity and dramatism (K. Mägi 1908, Juhan Liiv 1909, M. Levin, I. Menning and Viktoria Martna 1910). Later his manner of expression became more expressive compared to the earlier one (portrait of A. Laikmaa, 1913). The free graphic drawings "Martyr" (1913) and "Catastrophe" (1917) express the split personality and tragedy characteristic of expressionism. The drawings "The Birth of Freedom" (1919) and "Howling Dog" (1921) reflect the events following World War I. From the second half of the 1920s comes a number of drawn, distinctive portraits (F. Tuglas 1928, H. Raudsepp 1932, G. Suits 1933); in the 1930s he again cultivated landscape painting more, the most outstanding being landscapes made near Paldiski at Leetse-Lepiku and winter views of Tartu. He also created applied graphics (the publisher's mark of "Siuru", 1917) and the design of the Cross of Liberty (1920) (machine translation of the holder's Estonian text, unedited)
